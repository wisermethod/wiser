# Sequence Patterns

Reference material for the email sequences a funnel's stages run on. Covers five sequence types, the job each does, its cadence, the progression inside it, and how each one fails, then the criteria that map a campaign goal to a type and the timing principles that hold across all of them. `SKILL.md` consults this file when specifying an email stage; this skill is the library's single home.

Every count, interval, and cadence named here is a general industry pattern, unverified against any particular audience (`standards/conventions.md`). Test them against the real list; never assume them.

## Welcome

**Job:** turn a new subscriber into an engaged reader who trusts the sender.

**Cadence:** 5 to 7 messages over 10 to 14 days.

**Progression**

1. Immediately: deliver the thing that was promised, introduce who you are in two or three sentences, and set expectations for what arrives next.
2. Day 1 to 2: the origin story or the core belief. Why does this business exist, and what did someone run into that led here? This is where narrative connection forms.
3. Day 3 to 4: unexpected value. Teach something useful they did not expect, which breaks the opt-in-then-pitch pattern they are bracing for.
4. Day 5 to 7: proof. A case study, a testimonial, a concrete result; not that the product is good, but what happened for someone like them.
5. Day 8 to 10: introduce the core offer naturally, as the way to go further with what they have been reading.
6. Day 12 to 14: a soft close, with a specific and genuine reason to act now.

**Fails when** it pitches in the first message. The subscriber arrived seconds ago, for one specific reason. Honor that trade before asking for another.

## Nurture

**Job:** hold engagement with subscribers who are not ready to buy, so the brand is present when the timing changes.

**Cadence:** ongoing, one to two messages per week.

**Progression:** alternate education, story, and curated value; a soft mention of the offer belongs in roughly every fourth or fifth message. Segment on engagement, so the engaged get more and the disengaged go to a re-engagement sequence rather than absorbing more of the same.

**Fails when** every message asks for something. Nurture builds a relationship; a list that is only ever sold to unsubscribes, and the ones who stay stop opening.

## Launch

**Job:** convert inside a defined window, with momentum the individual messages could not produce alone.

**Cadence:** 5 to 10 messages over 7 to 14 days.

**Progression**

1. Days minus 3 to minus 1: build anticipation. Tease what is coming and name the problem it solves.
2. Launch day: announce. Value proposition, social proof, one action. This message gets the most opens of the sequence.
3. Day 2 to 3: answer the single biggest objection, directly.
4. Day 4 to 5: proof, in the form of a case study or a testimonial from someone recognizably like them.
5. Day 6 to 7: the remaining objections, as a set. Remove what friction is left.
6. Day 8 to 9: a scarcity reminder, only where it is real (spots remaining, a price that genuinely changes).
7. Final day: last call. Direct, short, no ornament.

**Fails when** the deadline is fictional. A launch that closes and reopens teaches the audience to ignore every future deadline, which costs more than the sales the fake one won.

## Re-engagement

**Job:** recover subscribers who stopped opening, or clean them off the list.

**Cadence:** 3 to 4 messages over 7 to 10 days.

**Progression**

1. Name the silence and give a reason to stay: the best recent piece, or a new resource.
2. Ask what they want. A reply, or a click that picks a topic, which segments the ones who remain.
3. Make the stakes plain: the list is being cleaned, and staying takes one click.
4. Optional final notice, stating that this is the last message unless they act.

Remove the non-responders when the sequence ends. A clean list improves deliverability for everyone still on it.

**Fails when** it guilt-trips. "We miss you" is manipulation dressed as warmth; state the facts, offer the value, and let them decide.

## Onboarding

**Job:** get a new customer to value quickly, reducing churn by driving activation.

**Cadence:** 5 to 8 messages over 14 to 21 days.

**Progression**

1. Immediately: welcome, and the single most important first step. Not a feature tour; one action that produces the first moment of payoff.
2. Day 1 to 2: the second key action, built on the first, showing what is possible now that step one is done.
3. Day 3 to 5: the most common early friction point, named plainly, with the way past it.
4. Day 7: check in, and point at the support channels.
5. Day 10 to 14: an advanced use case, expanding what they think the product is for.
6. Day 14 to 21: hand off to regular engagement, the community, the newsletter, the ongoing resources.

**Fails when** it dumps features. Onboarding is not a manual; each message drives one action.

## Selection Criteria

| Campaign goal | Primary sequence | Why |
|---------------|------------------|-----|
| A new subscriber | Welcome | Build trust before selling anything |
| An ongoing relationship | Nurture | Stay present without burning the list down |
| A product or feature launch | Launch | Momentum and urgency inside a defined window |
| List hygiene | Re-engagement | Recover the recoverable, remove the rest |
| A new customer | Onboarding | Drive activation, which is what reduces churn |
| A launch to an existing list | Welcome for the new arrivals, Launch for the existing ones | Two audiences in different states need different sequences |

## Timing Principles

- **Respect inbox frequency.** More than three messages a week from one sender pushes most audiences toward unsubscribing. The exception is an active launch window, where daily sending is expected and forgiven.
- **Front-load value.** The first three messages of any sequence set the expectation for all of them. Three valuable ones buy attention for what follows; one piece of filler among them costs open rates that do not recover.
- **Match timing to decision complexity.** A low-consideration purchase can close in a three-day sequence. A high-consideration one, a consulting engagement or enterprise software, needs two to four weeks.
- **Test send times rather than assuming them.** Business audiences often engage inside working hours, midweek and mid-morning; consumer audiences often engage evenings and weekends. Both are starting hypotheses, not settings.
