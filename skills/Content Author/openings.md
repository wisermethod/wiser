# Openings

The first line earns the second. This file carries the craft of that line: a headline, a title, a subject line, the sentence a piece opens on. Read it with whichever content-type file applies, at the point in Draft where the opening gets written, whenever the reader arrives cold and has to be given a reason to continue. The type file says how long the opening can be and where the platform cuts it off; this file says what has to be inside it.

## What an opening has to do

Three forces at once. An opening carrying two of them is a sentence, not a hook.

**Specificity.** A concrete detail, number, or image the reader can hold. An abstract opening lets someone skim past without absorbing anything, so leaving costs them nothing.

**Recognition.** Name something the reader feels and has not put words to. The test is whether they would think "that is exactly it". Describing the situation is not the same as naming what it feels like from inside, and the difference is the whole effect.

**Incompleteness.** A question or tension the next line has to resolve. The reader should already be leaning into the second line while the first one finishes.

## The opening moves

Five ways in. Pick one and commit; they work by different mechanisms and stacking them produces noise rather than force.

**Specific.** Swap the vague claim for the concrete one: the figure, the date, the thing that can be pointed at. Specificity reads as credibility before the reader has decided whether to trust you.

**Provocative.** Contradict something the reader believes. The gap between their belief and the claim is what carries them forward, so it has to be a belief they actually hold; a contradiction of nobody's position lands on nobody.

**Action.** Start inside something happening rather than in the setup before it. Two beats of sequence create momentum that description cannot.

**Revealing.** Give them something they did not know and would want to. New information earns attention by itself, which makes it the move most damaged by a number nobody can source; what opens here carries its source and register per `standards/conventions.md`.

**Kinetic.** Put the rhythm in the opening itself: short then long, question then answer, setup then payoff. Variation is the audible sign that a person wrote this rather than a pattern.

## Emotional drivers

The move decides the shape of the opening. The driver decides what it pulls on. Choose one from the reader's relationship to the topic, and do not layer a second emotional framework over it.

- **Frustration.** Something is broken and costing them, and they have not been able to name it.
- **Ambition.** There is an edge they are missing: what the people ahead of them know, and what not knowing it has been costing.
- **Curiosity.** An information gap they will want closed.
- **Vindication.** Someone finally said what they suspected and could not defend.
- **Disgust.** A visceral reaction to hypocrisy, waste, or exploitation they have witnessed.

## Checks

Run all four. A failure is a rewrite, not a polish; the opening is the one line where a better word cannot save a weaker thought.

- **Recognition check.** Would the reader think "that is exactly it"? If not, the line described a situation without naming the experience inside it.
- **Pull check.** Does the first line make the second unavoidable? If they can stop there with nothing unresolved, it is not an opening.
- **Uniqueness check.** Swap in a different topic. If the line still works, it belongs to no piece in particular.
- **Subtext check.** Emotion is shown through the situation, never named. "This frustrating situation" spends the feeling the situation would have produced.

## What kills an opening

- Throat-clearing: context, credentials, an announcement of what the piece is about to do, or thanks to anyone.
- A line that could open any piece on any subject.
- Technically correct and emotionally flat.
- Cliche, or syntax packed so tight the reader has to read it twice to parse it.
- Slogan cadence, where a person talking belongs.

## When not to open on a hook

Reference documentation, technical specification, academic writing, internal notes, and anything a reader reached by searching for it: they arrived committed and want to find something, not to be held. Open on what the document is and what it covers. A hook in front of reference material reads as a pitch and costs the trust the material was going to earn.

An outreach email is the other exception, and for the opposite reason. Its reader is cold in the strict sense and still not hookable: their first question is who is writing and why this is in their inbox, and tension applied before that question is answered reads as a sales technique. `cold-email.md` owns that opening.
