# Blog Post

A web-published article, roughly 800 to 2,000 words, read by someone who clicked through from a search result, a feed, or an email and has not yet committed to finishing. The reader scans before reading, so the structure is doing work before the prose is. Read this file at Frame and hold it through Draft. Past roughly 2,000 words the piece stops being a web article and becomes an extended argument: `long-form.md`.

## Shape

- **Length.** 800 to 2,000 words, and 1,200 to 1,500 is where most subjects land. Under 800 the subject was thin or the piece is really a post. Over 2,000 needs a reason the reader can feel. Draft to about 1.3 times the target and cut back to it; the specificity survives a cut and the padding does not, which is not true of a draft written to the number the first time.
- **Sections.** An opening claim, then three to five body sections behind descriptive headers.
- **Paragraphs.** Two to three sentences. Screen reading breaks down around the fourth, and a wall of text is skipped rather than read slowly.
- **Stance.** First person where the claim comes from experience, plain third person elsewhere. The reader is a practitioner who holds the basics, so explaining a foundational concept reads as padding unless the piece is explicitly introductory.

## Format rules

- **Title.** Concrete, and about what the piece establishes rather than the subject it circles. A title that names the topic could sit on fifty other pieces; a title that names the finding could sit on one.
- **Headers.** Descriptive rather than clever, and valuable read alone. Someone who reads only the headers should come away with the spine of the argument.
- **Scan test.** Read the title, every header, and the first sentence under each. If that sequence does not tell a coherent story, the piece is failing the majority of its readers, who scan it in that order before deciding to read anything.
- **Links.** Cite sources inline where the claim is made, so a skeptical reader can check it without leaving the paragraph. No references section at the end unless the piece is documentation.
- **Lists.** A bulleted list earns its place when the items are short and genuinely parallel. A list whose items are paragraphs is prose that lost its transitions; write the transitions back in.
- **Search.** Where a target search phrase exists, it belongs in the title and in the first section if it fits the sentence naturally, and nowhere by force. A piece built around a phrase reads like one, and the reader who arrives on it leaves immediately, which is the outcome the phrase was chosen to prevent.

## Failure modes

- **Search-first writing.** Hitting a phrase and a word count while saying nothing. Test: would a practitioner send this to another practitioner? If not, it is inventory, not a piece.
- **Buried lead.** Three paragraphs of context before the point. On the web the reader is gone by the second. The claim opens the piece and the context follows it.
- **Unscannable wall.** Long paragraphs, vague headers, no structure. Test: can someone get value from 30 seconds of scanning? If not, the structure needs headers and breaks, not better sentences.
- **The interchangeable list piece.** Numbered tips with no thread between them, each obvious on its own. Test: could this exact piece exist on fifty other sites? Then find the angle that only you can write, or do not publish it.
- **Padding to the number.** Repeating a point with a second example, or elaborating something already clear, to reach a word count. If the piece is done at 900 words it is done, and the padding is what the reader remembers as thin.

## Format checks

- The opening lands the claim inside the first two or three sentences.
- Headers are descriptive and the scan test tells the story.
- Paragraphs run two to three sentences, with no wall of text.
- Sources are cited inline, at the claim.
- Length sits inside the band, and nothing in the piece is there to reach it.
