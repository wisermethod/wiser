# Cold Email

An outreach email to one person who never asked to hear from you, read on a phone among fifty others that morning. It covers the sequence, not one message: first contact, one follow-up, one close. Read this file at Frame and hold it through Draft. A persuasive document that asks someone to decide on an offer is `skills/Proposal Author/`.

## Shape

- **Length.** 50 to 125 words for the first email, 75 to 150 for the follow-up, 50 to 100 for the close. Every word is spent from a budget the reader did not agree to.
- **Structure.** Subject line, then four lines: connection, ask, credibility, question.
- **Stance.** Peer to peer, declarative, no storytelling. Someone offering something, not someone apologizing for existing.
- **Formatting.** One to three sentences a paragraph, no bullets in the body, since bullets are the visual signature of mass mail. A signature of name, role, and one line of context, and nothing else.

## Before the first email

Cold is usually avoidable. Where the recipient posts publicly, engage once or twice before the email lands so the name arrives familiar rather than unknown. The research behind one email costs five to ten minutes and decides whether it reads as addressed to them or to a list, which is the only thing that decides whether it is answered.

## Subject line

Boring wins. It should look like correspondence rather than a campaign: the recipient's first name, then the specific topic, under about fifty characters. An exciting subject line reads as marketing, and a vague one ("Quick question") reads as every other unread message in the inbox. Test: could this subject line go to a hundred people unchanged? Then it is not yet about them. No emoji, no capitals, no exclamation marks.

## The four lines

1. **Connection.** The specific, checkable thing you know about them: something they wrote, shipped, said, or changed. It has to connect to the ask, because a compliment that leads nowhere proves only that you can compliment.
2. **Ask.** What you want, stated plainly, once. One ask per email; a second one halves the first.
3. **Credibility.** One sentence on why you are worth a reply: the part of your background that bears on the ask, not the resume.
4. **Question.** End on a question that is easy to answer. A question leaves a loop the reader wants to close; a statement lets them close the tab instead.

## The sequence

Three emails at most, seven to ten days apart, then stop.

- **Follow-up.** Reference the first email in the first line, then add something that was not in it: a development, a narrower offer, a piece of context. Restate the ask more clearly than before, and close on a question that offers an exit ("or is this better after the quarter?"). A follow-up that only says you are following up spends goodwill and buys nothing.
- **Close.** The shortest of the three. Say you have reached out, offer them the reading that the timing is wrong rather than the reading that they were rude, hand them a graceful way out, and end on something genuine about their work. The point of the third email is that the relationship survives it.

## Format rules

- No attachment on first contact. It trips spam filtering and asks for a commitment nobody has made.
- No images or logos in the body, for the same reason.
- Plain text, from a personal address rather than a shared one, sent during business hours in their time zone. Never to a list, and never with the recipients visible to each other.
- No emoji in a professional cold email.
- Nothing that opens with an apology, a wish that the email finds them well, or an explanation of their own industry to them.

## Failure modes

- **The apology open.** "Sorry to bother you", "I know you're busy." It tells the reader that even the sender doubts the message deserves attention.
- **The founder's manifesto.** Three paragraphs about your company, your vision, and your journey before anything about them. They do not care yet, and none of that is what will make them.
- **The industry explainer.** Telling a stranger what their own industry is going through. It reads as presumption and is usually wrong in the specifics.
- **The vague ask.** "Let me know if you'd like to connect." It hands the reader the work of deciding what is being asked, and they will not do it.
- **False flattery.** "I love your work", followed by something generic, proves the research never happened.
- **The wall of text.** A message that looks like an essay on a phone screen is closed before it is read.
- **Template syndrome.** Personalization is not a merge field. If the body would survive swapping the recipient, it was not written to them.
- **Premature attachment.** A deck or a one-pager on first contact, selling before anything has been established.
- **The bump.** A follow-up carrying no new information, which teaches the reader that the next one will be the same.
- **The guilt trip.** Any suggestion in the final email that they owed a reply. They did not, and the version that says so quietly is the one that closes the door.

## Format checks

- Subject line is specific to this recipient and under about fifty characters.
- Each email sits inside its band, and each ends on a question.
- The connection line names something checkable and connects to the ask.
- One ask per email, stated plainly.
- No attachment, no images, no bullets, no emoji.
- The follow-up adds something new, and the close offers an exit.
