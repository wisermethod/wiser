# Keynote

## Scope

A keynote is a professional conference talk, industry event address, company all-hands, or high-stakes presentation delivered by a speaker positioned as an authority on the subject. The occasion exists so that one idea, carried by one voice, lands in one room.

## Length target

Duration range: 15 to 40 minutes. At 130 to 150 words per minute spoken, that is 1,950 to 6,000 words. Most keynotes land at 18 to 22 minutes, the single-idea conference shape, which is 2,340 to 3,300 words. Longer keynotes, 35 to 40 minutes, expand the build stage proportionally; the shorter stages hold roughly constant.

## Register

Authoritative without lecturing. Warm enough to hold the room, firm enough to land the claim. Peer-to-expert is the correct tone: the speaker addresses the audience as colleagues one step along a shared path, not as students. First-person density is medium-high when the material is direct experience, medium when it is synthesis of others' work. Humor is permitted; it is never the load-bearing move.

## Ritual beats

- **Opening.** A concrete scene, a question, or a specific image. Credentials and thank-yous do not open a keynote; the audience's attention is a loan, and the first line earns it.
- **Single central idea.** A keynote lives or dies on a single provable claim. Multiple parallel claims read as a panel or a seminar, not a keynote.
- **Evidence in layers.** The build moves story to principle to pattern, not principle to examples.
- **Signature phrase.** One line the audience repeats after leaving the room. Typically the throughline in its most compressed form.
- **Close.** A call to see differently or act differently. Not a thank-you, not a housekeeping note. Applause follows the last content sentence.

## Occasion-specific conventions

- The audience has chosen to be there; attention is assumed at the opening and does not need to be re-earned every ninety seconds.
- Slides, where present, are subordinate to speech. A keynote that only works with slides is not a keynote.
- Q&A, when scheduled, happens after. The close is still the close.
- Name-drops and affiliation mentions are allowed but sparse. They spend credibility rather than earning interest.
- Opening line patterns that hold across venues: a named scene ("It is the third week of the recall, and the returns bin by the loading dock has stopped emptying..."), a named question ("What does the hiring market know that founders do not?"), a named number paired with an image ("Sixty-eight days. That is how long a request sat in that queue before anyone read it...").
- Applause architecture: two to three engineered moments, typically at the end of the build and in the gift. The turn is rarely applause-shaped; it is often followed by silence, which is the correct response.

## Anti-patterns

- **Thanking the organizer before the opening line.** Steals the first eight seconds. If thanks are necessary, they live as a brief bookend after the close.
- **Three-part talks announced at the top** ("today I want to cover three things"). Reads as a syllabus. A single throughline with three supporting beats delivers the same content with higher retention.
- **Credential warm-up.** The audience already knows who the speaker is; that is why they came.
- **Generic applause lines** ("we must all do better," "the time is now"). Cost nothing, land nothing.
- **Data dumps.** A keynote is not a white paper. One number per minute is a high ceiling; each number earns its place through a concrete pairing.
- **The summary close** ("so to recap, the three things I covered..."). Spends the most valuable moment in the talk on backward-looking housekeeping.

## Notes on arc adaptation

The universal arc (empathy anchor to curiosity gap to build to turn to gift) applies without override. Stage weighting at an 18-minute length typically runs: empathy anchor 60 to 90 seconds, curiosity gap 60 seconds, build 10 to 12 minutes, turn 90 seconds, gift 90 seconds. At 35 to 40 minutes the build expands with additional evidence layers; the other four stages hold roughly constant. The long build is the occasion's distinguishing feature; a keynote without a sustained build is a panel remark in keynote clothing.
