# Stump Speech

## Scope

A stump speech is a repeatable core speech delivered across many rooms with minor variation: political campaigns, movement-building tours, book tours, mission-driven organization events, or any recurring speaking format where the same message must land in dozens of venues without drifting. The occasion's function is to plant a single message deeply enough that it survives across rooms, audiences, news cycles, and a tired speaker at the fourth event of the day.

## Length target

Duration range: 12 to 25 minutes. At 130 to 150 words per minute, that is 1,560 to 3,750 words. Most stump speeches land at 18 to 20 minutes (2,340 to 3,000 words). Length bends to the room; the core holds, the local hook and example beats flex. Repeatability matters more than precise duration.

## Register

Plain, rhythmic, repeatable. Oratorical where the occasion calls for it, conversational where it does not, but always with an internal pulse that makes the speech easy to deliver consistently, tired, in a loud room, without notes. First-person density is medium. The speech belongs to the movement or the mission more than to the speaker; "we" and "they" do more load-bearing work than "I."

## Ritual beats

- **Throughline stated early.** The one-sentence throughline appears inside the first two minutes.
- **Throughline restated.** Three to five times across the speech, each restatement in slightly different words. Repetition is the architecture; it is not accidental.
- **Signature phrase.** A single line, often four to seven words, repeated verbatim at each return. The phrase is the speech's fingerprint.
- **Call-and-response or refrain.** Applause architecture is explicit: two or three engineered moments, often structured as tricolons or antitheses, which the speaker hits at the same emotional beat every time.
- **Local hook.** One beat adapted to each venue: city, region, room, current event. Everything else holds.
- **The ask.** Specific, stated plainly, repeated at the close. What the room does next.

## Occasion-specific conventions

- **Repetition is the feature.** A stump speech that varies too much loses the movement's voice. A stump speech that varies too little goes stale for the speaker. The balance is: signature phrase and ask identical across venues; local hook and example beats variable.
- **Written for the ear of an unknown room.** Short sentences. Grounded nouns. Rhythms the speaker can hit at 9 p.m. after four other events.
- **Road-tested lines.** Call-and-response lines are tested live and kept if they land in three rooms or more. Lines that fail across three rooms are cut, regardless of how good they read on paper.
- **The close is the ask, every time.** Ambiguity at the close bleeds action. If the room cannot state what was asked after leaving, the close has failed.
- **Cross-venue consistency.** The signature phrase and the ask are word-for-word identical across rooms. The opening hook, the example beats, and some proof points rotate.
- **Applause architecture.** Deliberate. Two or three engineered moments across the speech. Tricolon cascades and antithesis lines are built in and protected through every revision.
- **Humor policy.** Humor in a stump is a repeatable asset, not a one-off flourish. Lines are road-tested the same way call-and-response lines are: a joke that lands in three rooms enters the stump; a joke that fails in three rooms exits. The humor that survives is the humor the speaker can deliver at the same emotional beat in every venue: a dry observation about the opposition's conventional wisdom, a recognizable local counter-example, an ironic aside built to hit twice in a tour. Political and movement rhetoric relies on this kind of humor; it is built, tested, and protected like any other engineered moment.
- **Opening line patterns** that hold: entering on the local hook ("I drove in from the next county this morning..."), entering on the signature phrase ("There is a promise this country keeps breaking, and we are here to name it."), entering on the day's news made personal ("Yesterday the number came out, and I want to tell you what that number looks like from my kitchen table."). Entering on the speaker's own arrival pleasantries wastes the room.
- **Delivery consistency.** The speech is written so that the same emotional beat lands at the same point in every delivery. Off-script variation lives in the local hook, not in the engineered moments.

## Anti-patterns

- **The one-off flourish.** A brilliant line invented for one room does not belong in the stump; it belongs somewhere else (op-ed, one-off keynote, press statement).
- **The news-cycle hijack.** Bending the throughline to each day's headline erodes the throughline within a week.
- **Over-adaptation.** A stump speech tailored beyond the local hook stops being a stump.
- **Under-adaptation.** Zero local reference reads as phoning it in.
- **Burying the ask.** The close is the ask; anything after the ask dilutes it.
- **Running past the peak.** The speech ends on the last engineered moment, not ten minutes after. Every minute past the peak applause subtracts from it.
- **Abandoning the signature phrase mid-tour.** The phrase is the inheritance of repetition; cutting it resets the counter and re-educates the movement.
- **Script creep.** Each event adds a sentence; after twenty events, the speech has grown by five minutes and lost its shape. Periodic returns to the road-tested core are preventive.

## Notes on arc adaptation

The universal arc applies, with structural repetition layered over it. At 18 to 20 minutes the typical stage weighting runs: empathy anchor (local hook plus first statement of the throughline) 2 to 3 minutes; curiosity gap (what the throughline demands from the room) 1 to 2 minutes; build (three or four proof beats, each closing on a restated version of the throughline) 10 to 12 minutes; turn (the engineered antithesis line) 1 to 2 minutes; gift (the ask plus signature phrase, stated plainly) 1 to 2 minutes. The arc is the frame; repetition is the texture laid across it. Unlike other occasions, the signature phrase may surface in every stage. The build is the longest stage; the turn is the shortest but structurally load-bearing.
