# Presentation Structure

How to arc a presentation for impact. Structure is the difference between a collection of slides and a story that moves people. Read this at the Calibrate and Design-the-arc steps.

## The Core Principle

Every presentation is an argument. The structure should make the conclusion feel inevitable, not announced.

## Calibration

Confirm four parameters before choosing an arc. Each shapes a different part of the deck.

| Parameter | Options | Default | Effect |
|-----------|---------|---------|--------|
| Type | pitch, technical, educational, strategy, general | general | Selects the structural arc below |
| Duration | 5, 15, 30, 45, 60 min | 15 min | Sets the slide-count range |
| Context | live, screen share, PDF, self-guided | screen share | Sets text density; self-guided decks carry more on-slide text, live talks less |
| Tone | formal, conversational, technical, inspirational | conversational | Sets register and word choice |

## Opening the Presentation

The first sixty seconds decide whether the audience pays attention for the next thirty minutes. Every arc starts with a hook.

Strong opens:

- **Surprising fact.** A number or claim from the source material that contradicts what the audience expects, carrying its source on the slide. Never open on a figure you cannot attribute.
- **Specific scenario.** Put the audience in a situation. "Imagine you just got a message from your biggest customer. They are leaving."
- **Provocative question.** Ask something the audience cannot answer comfortably. "When was the last time your onboarding process changed?"
- **Contrast.** Show the gap between expectation and reality. "We planned for 10,000 users. We got 340,000."

Weak opens, to avoid:

- Self-introduction or credentials listing. The audience does not care who you are until they care what you have to say.
- An agenda slide first. Use it second, after the hook.
- "Thank you for having me" or "I'm excited to be here." These are filler. Start with the content.
- Apologizing for anything, the topic, the slides, or the time.

## Structural Arcs by Use Case

Select the arc that matches the deck's purpose. Each arc defines a sequence of narrative beats, not individual slides; a single beat may span one to five slides.

### Business Pitch

Goal: secure a decision (investment, partnership, purchase).

1. **Hook.** A surprising fact, provocative question, or vivid scenario that makes the audience feel the problem.
2. **Problem.** Define the pain, specific and quantifiable. The audience should think "yes, that is exactly right."
3. **Stakes.** What happens if the problem persists. Create urgency without fear-mongering.
4. **Solution.** Your approach, positioned as the natural resolution to the tension you built.
5. **Proof.** Evidence that it works: case studies, data, testimonials. Match the proof type to the audience's skepticism.
6. **How it works.** Enough detail to build credibility, not so much it becomes a tutorial.
7. **Ask.** A clear call to action. What you want them to do, and what happens next.

### Technical Talk

Goal: transfer knowledge and build credibility.

1. **Context.** Why this topic matters right now. Connect to something the audience already cares about.
2. **Problem statement.** The specific technical challenge, stated precisely.
3. **Prior art.** What exists, what works, what does not. Show you did the homework.
4. **Approach.** Your solution. Explain the key insight, not just the implementation.
5. **Demo or walkthrough.** Show it working: code, architecture, a live demo.
6. **Trade-offs.** What you gained and what you gave up. An honest assessment builds trust.
7. **Takeaways.** What the audience should do with this knowledge.

### Educational or Workshop

Goal: enable the audience to do something new.

1. **Why this matters.** Motivation, connected to the learner's goals.
2. **Current state.** Where the audience is today. Acknowledge their existing knowledge.
3. **Concept introduction.** One new concept at a time: define, explain, illustrate.
4. **Guided practice.** Walk through an example together.
5. **Common mistakes.** What goes wrong and how to recover. This is where trust is built.
6. **Independent application.** A challenge or exercise for the audience.
7. **Summary and resources.** Reinforce the key points and give next steps for continued learning.

### Strategy or Board Deck

Goal: align stakeholders and secure approval.

1. **Executive summary.** The entire story on one slide. Decision-makers may not stay for the whole deck.
2. **Context.** Market conditions, competitive landscape, internal situation.
3. **Opportunity or threat.** What prompted this strategy. Make it concrete.
4. **Recommended approach.** What you propose and why.
5. **Supporting analysis.** Data, models, scenarios: enough to validate, not to overwhelm.
6. **Resource requirements.** What it costs in time, money, and people.
7. **Risk assessment.** What could go wrong and how you will mitigate it.
8. **Decision request.** Exactly what you need approved.

### General or Flexible

When the deck fits no specific type, use the universal structure.

1. **Open.** Earn attention with a hook, context, or surprising insight.
2. **Setup.** Establish the framework. What is this about and why now.
3. **Body.** Three to five main points, each with evidence.
4. **Turn.** The "so what" moment. Synthesize the points into a larger insight.
5. **Close.** A clear takeaway and call to action.

## Closing the Presentation

The close is the last thing the audience hears. It decides what they remember and whether they act.

Strong closes:

- **Callback to the open.** Return to the hook. If you opened with a problem, show it solved; if with a number, show the new number.
- **Single clear ask.** One sentence. "Approve the budget by Friday." "Try the API this afternoon." "Share this with your team lead."
- **Forward-looking statement.** Paint what happens next if they act, a specific near-term outcome, not a vague vision.

Weak closes, to avoid:

- "Any questions?" as the final slide with no preceding summary. Summarize first, then invite questions.
- Restating every point. The summary is one slide, not a recap of the whole deck.
- New information in the close. If it matters, it belongs in the body.
- A "thank you" slide with no contact information or next step. If they want to follow up, make it easy.

## Audience Calibration

The same content needs different structural emphasis for different audiences.

| Audience | Emphasize | De-emphasize |
|----------|-----------|--------------|
| Executives | Summary, decision, ROI, timeline | Technical detail, methodology, implementation |
| Technical | Architecture, trade-offs, code, reproducibility | High-level vision, branding, org politics |
| Investors | Market size, traction, team, unit economics | Product minutiae, implementation details |
| Customers | Value to them, proof from peers, ease of adoption | Internal process, technical architecture |
| Partners | Mutual benefit, integration points, shared audience | Internal metrics, proprietary methods |
| Learners | Concepts, examples, practice, common mistakes | Competitive positioning, sales, internal jargon |
| Mixed | Layered detail, overview first with optional drill-down | Jargon in either direction, assumed shared context |

## Slide Count Guidelines

Starting points, not rigid rules. Adjust for density and delivery context.

| Duration | Approximate slides | Notes |
|----------|--------------------|-------|
| 5 min | 8 to 12 | Lightning talk. One idea. |
| 15 min | 15 to 25 | Focused topic. Tight pacing. |
| 30 min | 25 to 40 | Full presentation. Room for depth. |
| 45 to 60 min | 35 to 60 | Keynote length. Include breathing room. |
| Workshop | Varies | Dense sections need more slides at a slower pace. |

The "one slide per minute" rule is a rough average. Some slides stay up thirty seconds (transitions, images); others stay three minutes (a data walkthrough, a code review).

## Pacing Rhythm

Alternate high-density and low-density slides to hold attention.

High-density slides (text, code, data tables) ask the audience to read and process. Limit to two or three in a row before a break.

Low-density slides (single statements, images, section dividers) give a cognitive break. Use them between dense sections.

The pattern: dense, dense, breather, dense, dense, breather, section break. Never stack more than three consecutive dense slides without a visual or cognitive break.

## Beat Transitions

The connection between beats matters as much as the beats. A deck that jumps from Problem to Solution with no bridge feels disjointed.

Transition techniques:

- **Question bridge.** End a beat with a question the next beat answers. "So what does this cost us?" before the data beat.
- **Stakes escalation.** End with the consequence of inaction. "If we do nothing, here is where we land in twelve months." This pulls the audience into the solution beat.
- **Summary and pivot.** Briefly restate the key point, then redirect. "We have the evidence. The question is what to do with it." This works between any two beats.
- **Section divider slides.** A gradient-background divider between major beats signals a new chapter. Keep it to a headline and one line of context.

Transition anti-patterns:

- "Moving on to" or "Next, let's talk about" are verbal crutches, not transitions. The structure should make the progression feel natural.
- Abrupt topic changes with no connective tissue. If the audience has to context-switch, you lose them for ten to fifteen seconds.
- Repeating the agenda slide between every beat. Once at the start is enough; use section dividers instead.

Put transition cues in speaker notes, not on slides. The audience should feel the flow, not see the scaffolding.
